Cover Letter Guidance

Use these materials as you develop the cover letter that accompanies your resume.

Cover Letter: Design and Execution

The notes below explain the ideas I emphasize when discussing employment cover letters.

Beginning Caveat

Research has consistently shown that at least 1/3 but probably closer to ½ of recruiters and hiring managers do not read cover letters, preferring to go right to the resume. Does that mean that you should not write one? Absolutely not. Employment cover letters help you as a candidate to frame your skills, experiences and goals.

Employment Cover Letter Introduction

There are generally two cover letter families in professional and business writing: simple “transmittal-based letters” that may cover a report or proposal and summarize the document’s contents, and employment-based cover letters.

The most common kinds of cover letters are those associated with resumes or other job-search documents. A cover letter accompanying a report or proposal, for example, may just be a summary of that document. But an employment cover letter is more because it serves as part of a marketing package. Like the resume, employment cover letters must be persuasive as well. Their purpose is not to summarize what lies beneath them but, among other things, to persuade a reader to turn to the resume.

Rhetoric and Employment Cover Letters

Audience = the person who makes hiring decisions. But how do you identify that person?

One way is informational interview research. Identify the contact, suggest a brief meeting, use your SDSU status, buy a thank-you card, and prepare a highlighted version of your resume. You’d be surprised how many prospective employers will agree to an informal meeting or interview, particularly from students.

Consider taking the time to create such an opportunity and ask questions like:

  • What was your career path? How did you get involved in this field?
  • What is the biggest issue this industry faces at the moment?
  • How is your company reacting to this issue?
  • What do you look for in a potential candidate?
  • I’m near graduation. What would you do in my position?
  • I’m interested in getting more information. Can you suggest someone else I can talk to, and is it OK to keep you updated on my progress?
Purpose of the Cover Letter

Generally, the purpose of all cover letters is to introduce the subject as it is manifested in the document it covers. Employment cover letters specifically generate interest in your skills, education and experience and serve as a bridge between the job posting and the resume.

It can be tempting just to generate interest by content. Perhaps you have done your work and reviewed the samples. Perhaps you have great experience, studied abroad, or had great internships. You may also have more information than can fit on your resume.

But good cover letter writers use effective visual design as well. They understand that how a cover letter looks is almost as important as what it says. You need to tell the reader’s eyes where to go.

An important component of professional writing is visual and information design. Information can be chunked, bullet pointed when appropriate, and written in short paragraphs rather than presented as a wall of text.

Cover letters also:

  • Explain your experiences in a story-like format that works with the information provided in your resume
  • Show the employer that you are individualizing, or tailoring, the job application
  • Provide a sample of your written communication skills

It is the first document an employer sees, so it is often the first impression you will make.

Exigency/Genre: Part of an Integrated, Focused Campaign

One overriding fact about the context of employment cover letters—and you need to remember this, perhaps more than any other professional document—is that readers look to disqualify cover letters and resumes, especially in competitive job climates.

Employment Cover Letter Readers

Because they are read to disqualify, audience is important for employment documents. Employment documents must be able to convince the reader very quickly that what you are saying is true in order to be effective.

You must have done your homework about the company because only then are you prepared to directly communicate with the organization. You also need to understand something about how people read employment documents.

  • They read job letters in stacks.
  • They are tired.
  • They often do this reading late in the day and are less patient with flowery diction, hard-to-read type, or other poor design choices.
  • They are impatient.
  • They will not dig for information. They expect it to jump right out at them.
  • They suddenly become very picky proofreaders.
Contact Information

Provide your contact information. Include the date you are writing the letter and the address of the company.

Introduction

The first paragraph offers the way into the letter. It identifies who you are, what your purpose is, what posting you are responding to, where you saw the posting, anyone you know in the organization who has referred you, or it may offer a reason that captures your inspiration for applying.

The most important information should be summarized here. Provide a quick overview of your qualifications.

Greet the specific person with whom you are corresponding. You MUST find this person’s name for the final resume you turn in, even if it’s the CEO. If you don’t know the person, make a name up for the purposes of this class.

Employment Cover Letter Body

The body of the letter, which consists of more than one paragraph, is the most important part of the letter.

Be very aware of the letter’s purpose. The purpose of an employment cover letter is to amplify and explain skills and experience from the resume and match them to the job requirements in declarative statements.

Focus each paragraph on one qualification that shows you are a good match for the job and organization. Tell a story; do not just list your skills. Give specific examples to prove where you got these skills and how you have used them before.

Refer to your resume; do not repeat it. Use block paragraph format with bullets if appropriate. Let them know subtly that you know something about the company. Definitely use keywords from the job description.

Conclusion

Exit with confidence. Close with a strong reminder of why you are a good match for the job and the organization.

Present information about how the employer can contact you. Request an interview in some way or at the very least make scheduling an interview easy. Thank the person for reading your material. Sign your name and print it underneath.

Summary: Employment Cover Letters

Consider the cover letter as part of a marketing package. Do your homework about the company, about discipline-specific communication types, and as much as you can about the reader. Consider yourself vital and let this come through with humility.
