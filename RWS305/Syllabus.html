Course Description

Successful completion of RWS 305W satisfies the Graduation Writing Assessment Requirement (GWAR) for students who have met the prerequisites noted above.

Rhetoric and Writing Studies 305W is designed to help upper level students develop the communication skills needed for successful professional careers. The course builds on the rhetorical model introduced in earlier 100 and 200 level courses: attention to audience, purpose, presentation through genre and organization, evidence, and language.

Student Learning Outcomes

In RWS 305W, students learn to respond not only to academic tasks but to a wider variety of genres and settings that require varied research methods and writing styles. The course also asks students to reflect on and evaluate the effectiveness of their own writing styles within rhetorical contexts.

  • Analyze and evaluate complex print, digital, and multimodal texts that engage significant academic, professional, or civic issues.
  • Apply rhetorical principles appropriate to different purposes and goals within specific disciplinary, professional, and civic communities.
  • Research and contribute to specific areas of inquiry by evaluating, synthesizing, and integrating strategies and sources appropriate to genre.
  • Adapt and employ conventions to communicate with diverse audiences who are members of or affected by a specific area or discipline.
  • Compose a variety of texts, working individually and collaboratively, through processes of drafting, critiquing, reflecting, and editing.

Assignment and Assessment Methods

The course consists of four major projects arranged in modules, each responding to a different rhetorical situation. Within each module, a revised final draft of the project develops from a series of assignments, including reflection oriented journal entries informed by module readings, recorded video sessions, and working drafts.

Point values are based on the instructor’s judgment of the overall quality of submitted coursework as evaluated against published criteria. Due dates, module details, and submission instructions appear in the course assignment schedule on Canvas. Due dates are critical. All work is sequenced to support completion of the course learning outcomes. Students must assume a high level of self discipline and responsibility for meeting deadlines. Late submissions may not receive points.

Assignment Descriptions

There are four major projects in this class, along with smaller assignments intended to support understanding of the course materials and content.

Introductory Module: Syllabus and Policy Acknowledgment

The initial assignment familiarizes you with course goals and guidelines. By submitting the acknowledgment, you agree to honor all course policies.

Project 1: Career Portfolio

This module has three parts: a resume, a cover letter, and a reflection on career related readings. The resume and cover letter are written in response to a current job announcement. The readings support preparation of the documents and future job or internship applications.

Project 2: Labor Market Analysis Report

This module includes the Labor Market Analysis Report, an online quiz on plagiarism and paraphrasing, and two reflections: one on strengths and one on areas for improvement. For the report, you will research the skills, knowledge, and training needed for your future career, along with future salary and employment projections.

Project 3: Rhetorical Analysis Paper

This module includes three chapter quizzes, a reflection, and a rhetorical analysis paper. You will read Meg Jay’s “Identity Capital,” “My Life Should Look Better on Facebook,” and “Weak Ties.” The quizzes hold you accountable for the readings before you write. The paper asks you to analyze an author’s argument, rhetorical strategies, appeals, assumptions, and persuasiveness within a specific rhetorical context.

Project 4: Public Voice Project

For the final project, you will choose one of two options: the Public Discourse Project or the Future Retirement Speech. Both options ask you to use course materials to address an audience beyond this class.

The Public Discourse Project asks you to advise entering SDSU students about a major, career field, and preparation for future employability. The Future Retirement Speech asks you to speak from the point of view of your future self at the end of a career or major professional chapter.

Google Doc Policy

All work for every assignment in this course must be completed entirely within one Google Document created specifically for that assignment. You must give mfeigner@sdsu.edu full Editor access from the moment the document is created.

  • Each assignment, including the cover letter, resume, reflections, Labor Market Analysis Report, Rhetorical Analysis Paper, presentation script, and other written work, requires its own separate Google Doc.
  • All research, drafting, revising, editing, thinking, and development of the assignment must occur exclusively inside that document.
  • Using multiple Google Docs for the same assignment is prohibited.
  • All submissions must be made by sharing the single complete Google Doc with Editor access through the submission link on Canvas.

Failure to follow any part of this policy will result in an automatic zero on the assignment. This grade is final and nonnegotiable. No revision or makeup opportunity will be allowed for a policy violation.

By submitting work in this course, you acknowledge and agree to comply with this policy.

Academic Honesty

The University prohibits cheating and plagiarism. Examples of academic dishonesty include, but are not limited to:

  • Copying, in part or in whole, from another person’s test or examination.
  • Obtaining copies of a test, examination, or other course material without instructor permission.
  • Collaborating with others on work to be presented without instructor permission.
  • Falsifying records, laboratory work, or other course data.
  • Submitting work previously presented in another course when contrary to course rules.
  • Altering or interfering with grading procedures.
  • Assisting another student in any of the acts above.
  • Using sources verbatim or paraphrasing without proper attribution.
  • Copying and pasting work from an online or offline source and presenting it as your own.
  • Using information from an online or offline source without giving the author credit.
  • Replacing words or phrases from another source and inserting your own words or phrases.

The California State University system requires instructors to report all instances of academic misconduct to the Center for Student Rights and Responsibilities. Academic dishonesty will result in University disciplinary review and may lead to probation, suspension, or expulsion. Instructors may also penalize grades on work produced through academic dishonesty.

AI Generated Content Policy

For this course, Artificial Intelligence means any computer system or program that simulates, substitutes for, or enhances problem solving that typically requires human intelligence.

Students may use simple features resembling built in word processor tools for spelling and simple grammar checks. Students may not use generative AI at any stage of completing graded or ungraded work. Generative AI includes systems that ideate, summarize, generate, rewrite, or validate content, including tools such as ChatGPT, Bard, DALL E, Grammarly, Chegg, and similar programs when used for those functions.

Using an AI tool to create or aggregate course writing, reflective writing, or content that substitutes for or impersonates the student in a class activity constitutes academic misconduct similar to plagiarism or an honor code violation.

Exception requests: A student may request an exception in writing. The request must identify the assignment, the AI tool, the intended use, and the reason for the request. Requests are considered case by case.

Frequently Asked Questions

Questions About AI and Grading

Why did I get a zero on my work?

If you received a zero without an explanation in the comment box, it is almost certainly because you did not follow the Google Doc Policy in this syllabus.

Why can’t I have AI write my work and pretend it is my own? I will use AI in the workforce, so why can’t I use it now?

Firstly, the question is, on its face, dumb. To demonstrate that you are a proficient writer, you have to write. The instructor is here to guide and evaluate you, not another person and certainly not a robot.

The question also assumes that AI is a proficient writer. If proficient means mechanically correct, AI writing is often grammatical. That does not establish that it is readable, produces writing people want to read, or makes sound arguments. Those questions remain open for examination throughout the course.

More realistically, if AI offers anything useful to a writing process, you must first know how to write. You cannot judge good output from bad output without that knowledge. In this class, the instructor wants to see your writing and your thinking.

What happens if you suspect me of using AI for an assignment?

The submission will first be evaluated and scored according to the rubric. The instructor will then gather and document evidence and file a report with Student Rights and Responsibilities. That office will initiate an investigation, notify the student of the charge, conduct a fact finding process, and determine responsibility.

If the student is found responsible for violating course and University policies, the student will receive a zero on the assignment and may fail the course.

Why did you refer me to Student Rights and Responsibilities for academic dishonesty?

First, instructors are required to report academic misconduct to Student Rights and Responsibilities. The reporting process supports due process under Executive Order 1098.

  1. AI generated content has no place in a writing class when it is presented as student work. It complicates evaluation and wastes instructor time.
  2. Student Rights and Responsibilities records and tracks academic misconduct to support the integrity and rigor of the academic process and SDSU’s reputation.
  3. Unless the instructor is interacting with a chatbot, the instructor dislikes reading AI generated writing. Submitting it requires the instructor to read material the instructor did not ask for and does not want.

Questions About Canvas

How do I see feedback on my work?

Written feedback on Canvas appears in comments and annotations. Comments are external to the document. Annotations appear on the document itself.

Click the submitted document and hover over the comment bubbles to view annotations. In Google Docs, feedback appears as comments. Do not delete instructor comments until the semester concludes.

Questions About Due Dates

My internet went out before I submitted. I missed the deadline while finding another computer. Will this be a problem?

Due dates and submission times matter in this online course. For some assignments, a submission one or two days late may still be accepted without a grade reduction. You do not need to email the instructor when submitting within that grace period. Canvas may label the work late, but the alert may be ignored when the work falls within the stated grace period.
