Building a Recommendation from Evidence

Use research to reach factor-level conclusions, then connect those conclusions to the recommendation your report is built to support.

Research is only the beginning

A Recommendation Report needs more than useful sources. The report has to explain what the evidence means for the decision. That movement from evidence to meaning is analysis.

1. Factor question

Start with one factor your group said it would investigate.

2. Evidence

Bring together the strongest information that actually bears on that factor.

3. Analysis

Explain patterns, differences, limits, tradeoffs, and why the evidence matters.

4. Factor conclusion

State what the evidence now suggests about that part of the decision.

A source is not a section.

Your report should not become a sequence of “Source A says…” paragraphs. Organize the discussion around the factor being investigated and use multiple sources inside that discussion when they help answer the same question.

Evidence

What did the source actually establish?

  • A number, trend, finding, policy, comparison, or documented event
  • Something you can verify and cite

Analysis

Why does that evidence matter for the factor?

  • What it strengthens or weakens
  • How it connects with other evidence

Conclusion

What does the factor now suggest?

  • A judgment grounded in the evidence
  • Not simply another source summary

Build the analysis around the factor

Each factor should function like a research question. The evidence belongs together because it helps answer that question, not because the sources happen to be about the same company or industry.

Example factor: Is there enough customer demand to support another hotel near Snapdragon Stadium?
Evidence 1
Local demographic or visitor data shows who is in the market and when demand is likely to occur.
Evidence 2
Industry data shows whether hotel demand, occupancy, or room supply is growing, stable, or weakening.
Evidence 3
Current reporting or competitor information shows what nearby hotels are doing and whether new supply is already entering the market.
Analysis
The sources become useful when the report compares them: whether the local audience is large enough, whether broader lodging conditions support expansion, and whether nearby competition changes the opportunity.
Factor conclusion
The conclusion answers the demand question using the combined evidence. It can support the proposal, weaken it, or show that the answer depends on a condition the group still needs to address.
Compare

Do the sources point in the same direction? If not, what explains the difference?

Connect

How does one piece of evidence change the meaning of another?

Interpret

Why does the evidence matter to the organization, audience, or decision?

Qualify

What uncertainty, limitation, condition, or tradeoff keeps the conclusion from being stronger?

When the evidence does not line up neatly

Conflicting evidence is not a research failure. It often gives you the analysis. The useful question is why the sources differ and which evidence applies most directly to the decision your group is investigating.

Are the sources measuring the same thing?

Revenue growth, customer interest, actual purchases, and survey intentions can describe different parts of the same market.

Are they describing the same place or population?

National evidence may not describe San Diego, a campus population, or a specific customer segment.

Are they equally current?

A strong older study may explain behavior while current reporting shows that the market has changed.

What kind of source is speaking?

A company, regulator, trade association, journalist, database, and scholar may have different access to evidence and different purposes.

Does one source apply more directly?

A broad industry statistic can be accurate while a local source is still more useful for a location-specific recommendation.

Can the conclusion be conditional?

Mixed evidence may support a recommendation only under certain costs, locations, customer levels, policies, or implementation choices.

Evidence-to-analysis lab

Use this after researching one factor. The lab does not write the analysis for you. It organizes the evidence and your own interpretation into a factor-level research outline you can use while drafting.

Build a factor conclusion

Enter the evidence in your own words. Keep enough detail to remember what the source actually establishes.

For every evidence note: Name the source, then record the actual number, finding, policy, comparison, or documented event. Do not enter only the article title or database name. Use at least two different sources.

What pattern do you see?

Do not repeat the evidence. Explain what the pieces mean together and why that matters for the factor.

Identify an important limitation, uncertainty, condition, or tradeoff. Do not enter “None” or “N/A.” If no limitation is obvious, identify information you do not have yet or an assumption that could be wrong.

This is your factor-level conclusion. Answer from the evidence. Do not use “I think,” “we believe,” or personal preference. If you chose Incomplete, your conclusion may be that the evidence is not strong enough yet; state what still needs to be researched.

Move from factor conclusions to the recommendation
Once the group has conclusions for the important factors, put those conclusions next to each other. The recommendation should follow from their combined weight rather than from whichever source sounded most persuasive.
Question to askWhat you are deciding
Which factor is most decisive?Some evidence matters more to the decision than other evidence. A legal barrier, impossible cost, or lack of demand may outweigh several minor advantages.
Which factors support the change?Identify the conclusions that make the recommendation more workable, useful, profitable, efficient, equitable, or strategically sound.
Which factors create problems?Identify costs, risks, weak demand, implementation problems, competitive pressure, legal constraints, or other evidence working against the proposal.
Can the problems be managed?A limitation may change the form of the recommendation instead of killing it. Conditions, scope, timing, location, or implementation may matter.
What does the evidence support now?The final recommendation should reflect the strongest conclusion the evidence actually supports, not the position the group hoped to prove at the beginning.
Your recommendation can become more specific as you research.
Research may support the proposed change as originally imagined, narrow it, add conditions, change the timing or location, or reveal that a different version of the change is more defensible.


Quick check before drafting
A strong research process should leave you with more than a folder full of sources. You should know what each factor now suggests and why.
The discussion is organized by factors or decision questions.
Source names do not become the structure of the report.
Important claims use evidence that actually bears on the decision.
Interesting facts are not automatically useful facts.
Multiple sources are synthesized when they answer the same factor.
The report compares and connects evidence instead of presenting isolated summaries.
Contradictions and limits are explained.
The analysis does not pretend the evidence is cleaner than it really is.
Each major factor reaches a conclusion.
The reader should know what the evidence means for that part of the decision.
The recommendation follows from the factor conclusions.
The final position should be traceable back to the research rather than appearing suddenly at the end.
Finish line: You are ready to draft when you can explain what the strongest evidence means for each important factor and how those factor conclusions shape the recommendation.
