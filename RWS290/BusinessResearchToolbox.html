Use the source for the question it can answer

The Research Navigator tells you where to start. This toolbox explains what those sources contain, what they are good for, and where they become a weak fit. You do not need to use every resource on this page. If a source does not cover your organization, market, or issue, do not force it. Return to the factor and try another source family.

Research by factor, not by database.

If you are investigating customer demand, collect customer evidence. If you are investigating financial feasibility, collect financial evidence. A database is useful only when the information inside it helps answer the factor your group is working on.

Industry research

Industry research tells you what kind of market an organization is operating in: how large it is, how it is changing, who competes in it, what pressures matter, and what normal performance looks like.

What is an industry?

An industry groups organizations that perform similar economic activities. The useful question is not merely “What business is this?” but “What industry report would describe the competitive and operating environment around this problem?”

NAICS and SIC codesFind the formal industry category used by government and business databases.
Classification
Use when

You need the formal industry name or code before searching IBISWorld, government data, company records, or other business databases.

What it gives you

  • NAICS is the current North American Industry Classification System used by U.S. statistical agencies.
  • SIC is an older classification system that still appears in some business and regulatory sources.
  • A code can help when everyday language and database language do not match.
Do not force a bad category.

A company can operate across more than one industry. Choose the classification that best matches the factor you are investigating.

Search NAICS ↗Search SIC ↗

Example

A Recommendation Report about whether a company should open a dog-grooming business is not helped much by searching only dogs. The industry language Pet Care Services or the appropriate NAICS category gives business databases a much better target.

IBISWorldIndustry size, growth, competition, external drivers, forecasts, and financial benchmarks.
Industry
Use when

You need to understand the industry around a proposal, compare industry conditions, identify major competitors, or establish financial and operating benchmarks.

Look for

  • Industry performance and outlook
  • Major companies and market share
  • Key external drivers
  • Competitive landscape
  • Cost structure and financial ratios
  • Products, services, customers, and demand conditions
Industry evidence is not company evidence.

An industry average can provide a benchmark, but it does not prove that one specific company has the same costs, margins, customers, or risks.

Trade associationsIndustry statistics, reports, member information, regulatory issues, and the industry's own perspective.
Industry perspective
Use when

An industry organization tracks the exact issue your group is researching or publishes statistics that broad databases do not provide.

Trade associations can provide

  • Industry reports and statistics
  • Member surveys
  • Regulatory updates
  • Operating standards and best practices
  • Industry arguments about legislation, labor, technology, or market conditions
Remember whose perspective it is.

A trade association represents an industry. Its data may be useful, but its interpretation of regulation, policy, or public controversy is not automatically neutral.

Examples

The Recording Industry Association of America publishes music-industry reports. The National Restaurant Association publishes restaurant research and industry data.

Market data
Use when

You need a statistic, market estimate, forecast, chart, or survey result that helps establish the scale or direction of an industry or market.

Good uses

Mergent Market AtlasPublic-company financials, ratios, segments, management, and related market data.
Public company
Use when

Your recommendation depends on the financial condition or business structure of an existing public company.

Look for

  • Income statement, balance sheet, and cash-flow data
  • Financial ratios
  • Business descriptions and segments
  • Executives and company profile information
  • Competitor or industry context when available
Do not use it just because the company is public.

If the factor is customer attitudes or local demand, a company-finance database may be secondary to consumer and market evidence.

SEC EDGAR10-Ks, 10-Qs, 8-Ks, and other filings from publicly reporting U.S. companies.
Public filings
Use when

You need the company's own legally required disclosures about financial results, risks, major events, leadership, or business operations.

Common filings

  • 10-K: annual report and detailed risk/business disclosures
  • 10-Q: quarterly financial update
  • 8-K: major events reported between regular filings
Company filings are primary company evidence.

They are valuable for what the company reports about itself. Pair important claims about consequences, customers, or public reaction with independent evidence when the factor requires it.

Useful move

In a long 10-K, search within the filing for the exact issue you are researching: labor, cybersecurity, regulation, supply chain, customer concentration, competition, debt, expansion, or another factor.

PrivCoPrivate-company profiles, ownership, funding, investors, estimates, and growth information.
Private company
Use when

You are researching a private company covered by PrivCo, especially a venture-backed or private-equity-backed company.

Access note: First-time SDSU users may need to create a free academic PrivCo account.

Possible evidence

  • Ownership and investors
  • Funding rounds
  • Estimated revenue and company size
  • Acquisitions or growth events
  • Company and competitor profiles
Private-company numbers may be estimates.

Read how the information is described and avoid presenting an estimate as if it were an audited public filing.

Form 990 can help with

  • Revenue and expenses

  • Executive compensation
  • Programs and mission

Company newsroom / investor relationsWhat the organization has announced, planned, claimed, or reported about itself.
Company source
Use when

You need the organization's official announcement, strategic explanation, product launch, investor presentation, policy statement, or response to an event.

Useful for

  • Dates and details of company announcements
  • Official statements and strategy
  • Product or service launches
  • Investor presentations and annual reports
  • Company responses to controversy or regulation
A newsroom is not independent reporting.

Use it to document what the company says or does. Use articles, regulators, market data, or other independent evidence when you need to evaluate whether the company's claim holds up.

Consumer and local-market research

Use these sources when the recommendation depends on who the customers or users are, what they spend, how large the market is, where people live, or whether one place differs from another.

StatistaMarket statistics, forecasts, consumer surveys, charts, and data from many published sources.
Consumer / market
Use when

You need a fast way to locate consumer statistics, brand-use data, market estimates, survey findings, or forecasts.

Ask before using a statistic

  • Who collected the original data?
  • Who or what was measured?
  • What country or market does it describe?
  • When was the data collected?
  • Does it answer your factor or merely sound related?
Open Statista guide ↗Consumer Insights ↗
National does not automatically mean local.

A national consumer survey may explain broad behavior while still being a weak answer to a recommendation about one neighborhood, city, campus, or customer segment.

SimplyAnalyticsLocal demographics, spending, consumer behavior, maps, ZIP Codes, Census Tracts, and market comparisons.
Local market
Use when

Your factor depends on a geographic market: where customers live, what households spend, how areas differ, or whether one location has a stronger customer base.

Good uses

  • Compare ZIP Codes or Census Tracts
  • Map demographic variables
  • Compare household spending categories
  • Explore consumer or market variables by place
  • Build tables and charts for local comparisons
Choose variables because they answer the factor.

A map full of demographics is not analysis. Decide which characteristics would actually change your recommendation before collecting them.

Open SimplyAnalytics guide ↗
U.S. Census / American Community SurveyPopulation, households, income, education, housing, employment, and demographics by geography.
Government data
Use when

You need demographic evidence for a place or population and want data from the federal statistical system.

Useful for

  • Population and age
  • Households and families
  • Income and poverty
  • Education
  • Housing
  • Employment and commuting
Match the geography.

City, county, ZIP Code Tabulation Area, Census Tract, and metropolitan area are different units. Make sure the geography you cite matches the market you are discussing.

Secondary consumer research

Government data is useful when you need to establish who lives in a market before combining that evidence with spending, survey, industry, or business data.

Consumer Expenditure SurveysHousehold spending, income, and demographic information from the Bureau of Labor Statistics.
Spending data
Use when

Your recommendation depends on what households spend money on and how spending differs across demographic groups.

Useful for

  • Household expenditures
  • Income
  • Spending categories
  • Demographic comparisons
Broad spending evidence needs interpretation.

The survey can show spending patterns, but your analysis still has to explain whether those categories reasonably connect to the market or factor you are investigating.

Use it with another source

Pair spending evidence with local demographics, market statistics, industry conditions, or consumer surveys when the recommendation needs a more specific market argument.

Digital behavior and technology adoption

Some Recommendation Report factors depend less on where customers live and more on how people use technology, media, ecommerce, advertising, platforms, and digital services.

EMARKETEREcommerce, advertising, media use, technology adoption, forecasts, and digital consumer behavior.
Digital consumer
Use when

Your factor depends on digital behavior: social media, ecommerce, online advertising, mobile use, platform adoption, or a technology trend affecting customers.

Look for

  • Adoption rates and forecasts
  • Media-use patterns
  • Ecommerce behavior
  • Advertising spending and channels
  • Technology use by demographic group
  • Industry and competitor examples
Technology evidence should answer a business question.

Do not collect platform statistics just because the topic mentions AI, TikTok, Instagram, automation, or another technology. Use the evidence to test adoption, customer behavior, implementation, cost, risk, or another actual factor.

Official does not mean complete.

An agency or company can tell you the official rule, policy, budget, or current practice. Your Recommendation Report may still need independent articles, user research, market evidence, or other sources to evaluate consequences and alternatives.

Strong research usually mixes source families

The highest-value research does not come from collecting five versions of the same kind of evidence. Different sources answer different parts of the factor.

Financial factor

Company financials or filings + industry benchmarks + current reporting about costs, financing, or risk.

Customer-demand factor

Consumer survey or market data + local demographics/spending when geography matters + current reporting or competitor evidence.

Industry factor

IBISWorld or another industry report + trade association evidence + current business/trade reporting.

Location factor

SimplyAnalytics or Census + local government/zoning + competitor or local business evidence.

Technology factor

EMARKETER or adoption data + current reporting + company/industry implementation evidence.

Risk or regulation factor

Official law/regulation + current reporting + trade/industry evidence + company disclosures when relevant.

The goal is not database variety for its own sake. The mix matters because a Recommendation Report has to connect different kinds of evidence to the same decision.

Before you keep researching

Use this quick check when the search process starts turning into random accumulation.

I know which factor this source helps answer.

If you cannot name the factor, the source may be interesting but irrelevant.

I know what kind of evidence this source contains.

Company, industry, consumer, geographic, official, current reporting, or another clear type.

I am not treating an industry average as a company fact.

Benchmarks provide context. They do not replace organization-specific evidence.

I checked who produced the information.

Company, regulator, trade association, journalist, database publisher, government agency, or researcher.

I checked the date and geography.

Old or national data may still be useful, but only if it actually applies to the recommendation.

I have more than one kind of evidence when the factor needs it.

Do not let one database become the entire research process.
